### Proof of Good Triple Condition
We are investigating when $P = y^x z^y x^z$ is a positive prime number $p$.

**Part 1: Structural Necessity (Why two factors must be 1)**
For $P$ to be a prime $p$, it must satisfy two properties: (1) $P > 1$, and (2) $P$ has no proper divisors other than 1.
Let $A = y^x$, $B = z^y$, and $C = x^z$. Then $P = A \cdot B \cdot C$.
If $A, B, C$ are integers (which they must be if $P$ is prime), and $A, B, C > 0$, for $P$ to be prime, exactly one of the factors ($A, B, C$) must be equal to $p$, and the other two must be equal to 1.
If two or more factors were prime, the product would be composite. If any factor were composite (e.g., $A=4$), $P$ would be composite unless the other factors were fractional and canceled the composite nature, which is impossible if $P$ is an integer prime.

Therefore, a necessary condition is that *two* of the factors $\{y^x, z^y, x^z\}$ must evaluate to 1, and the remaining factor must evaluate to a prime $p$.

**Part 2: Analysis of $a^b = 1$ (The boundary conditions)**
The condition $a^b = 1$ allows for several cases:
1. **Trivial Case 1:** $a=1$ (for any integer $b$).
2. **Trivial Case 2:** $b=0$ (for any $a \neq 0$).
3. **Alternating Case:** $a=-1$ and $b$ is an even integer.

We must also address non-integer/zero results:
*   If any base is 0:
    *   $0^b = 0$ if $b > 0$. This leads to $P=0$, which is not prime.
    *   $0^b$ is undefined if $b < 0$. We must assume $b \ge 0$ if the base is 0.
    *   $0^0$: Depending on the convention, if $0^0=1$, this satisfies the required condition for a factor to be 1.

**Part 3: Checking Specific Boundary Triples (x, y, z $\in \{0, 1, -1\}$)**
We seek triples where two factors equal 1 and one factor equals a prime $p$.

1. **The Positive Case:**
   If $x, y, z \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, we found that the good triples must be permutations of $(p, 1, 1)$, where $p$ is prime. For example, $(1, p, 1)$ yields $p^1 \cdot 1^p \cdot 1^1 = p$. Here, $1^p=1$ and $1^1=1$.

2. **The Zero/One Boundary Cases:**
   *   Consider $(0, 1, p)$ where $p$ is prime.
      $P = 1^0 \cdot p^1 \cdot 0^p$. Since $p$ is prime, $p \ge 2$, so $0^p=0$. $P=0$. (Not a good triple).
   *   Consider $(p, 0, 1)$ where $p$ is prime.
      $P = 0^p \cdot 1^0 \cdot p^1$. Since $p \ge 2$, $0^p=0$. $P=0$. (Not a good triple).
   *   Consider $(1, p, 0)$ where $p$ is prime.
      $P = p^1 \cdot 0^p \cdot 1^0$. $P=0$. (Not a good triple).

3. **The Negative Boundary Cases (Required by the prompt):**

   *   Example: Let $x=-1$. We require one factor to be $p$ and two factors to be 1.
      *   If $y=2$ and $z=2$. $P = 2^{-1} \cdot 2^2 \cdot (-1)^2 = 1/2 \cdot 4 \cdot 1 = 2$.

      Since $y^x$ is not an integer, $P$ must be an integer. For $P$ to be an integer, any term $a^b$ where $b<0$ must have its denominator canceled out by the other terms, which is highly restrictive and generally only possible if the fractional part is $1/p$ or $p/1$, etc.

**Conclusion:**
The mathematical requirement that $P$ is a positive prime integer $p$ forces the structural condition: exactly one factor $A, B, C$ must equal $p$, and the other two must equal 1.
The boundary analysis shows that for integer results, the cases involving zero bases/exponents typically lead to $P=0$ (if the prime base is $\ge 2$), unless we strictly adhere to the convention $0^0=1$.
