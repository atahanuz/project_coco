### [W3, round 1]
Analysis of Minimal Overlap / Maximum Coverage:
The coloring occurs within a $75 \times 75$ square. We are looking for the maximum number of non-overlapping $75 \times 75$ squares that can fit within an $n \times n$ grid.
Let $N_{sq}$ be this number.
$N_{sq} = \lfloor n/75 \rfloor^2$.
As $n \to \infty$, the asymptotic behavior is $N_{sq} \sim (n/75)^2 = \frac{1}{5625} n^2$.

In the case of minimal overlap, the total number of green cells $G$ is bounded by the number of non-overlapping regions multiplied by the number of new cells colored per region ($s$).
$G \le N_{sq} \cdot s$.
Therefore, the maximum possible growth rate for $G$ under minimal overlap is $O(n^2)$.

This establishes the maximum growth rate, which is often used to determine the *minimum possible growth rate* that violates the sparsity condition $G \le Cn$. If the system can achieve $O(n^2)$ growth, it is definitively not sparse for any $s>0$.

The sparsity condition requires that the maximum number of green cells, $G_{\text{max}}(n, s)$, grows at most linearly with $n$, i.e., $G_{\text{max}}(n, s) = O(n)$, for every positive integer $n$. The operation allows coloring $s$ new cells within a fixed $75 \times 75$ region centered at an existing green cell $c$.

In this regime, the $75 \times 75$ operation covers the entire $n \times n$ grid or a significant portion of it. Assuming an optimal strategy where we select green cells $c$ such that the newly colored cells are uncolored and allow for maximum spread (e.g., choosing neighbors), the total number of green cells is primarily limited by the grid size $n^2$.

For $s=1$: Since we can spread one cell at a time, we can potentially color every cell.
$G_{\text{max}}(n, 1) \approx n^2$.
Scaling: $G_{\text{max}}(n, 1) = \Theta(n^2)$.

For $s=2$: Since we color 2 cells per turn, and we can spread effectively, the total count approaches the total number of cells.
$G_{\text{max}}(n, 2) \approx n^2$.
Scaling: $G_{\text{max}}(n, 2) = \Theta(n^2)$.

For $s=3$: Similarly, $G_{\text{max}}(n, 3) \approx n^2$.
Scaling: $G_{\text{max}}(n, 3) = \Theta(n^2)$.

In general, for $n \le 75$, $G_{\text{max}}(n, s) = \Theta(n^2)$. Since $n^2$ grows faster than $Cn$ for large $n$, these values of $s$ do not satisfy the sparsity condition for small $n$.

**Case 2: Large Grid ($n > 75$)**
In this regime, the operation is localized to a $75 \times 75$ area. Starting from a single initial cell, the set of reachable green cells is bounded by the connected area generated by the repeated $75 \times 75$ centered squares. Since the centers of the green cells must themselves be within the grid, and the maximum distance a green cell can spread from the initial seed is limited by the $75 \times 75$ radius, the total number of cells that can ever be colored is bounded by the size of the fixed operational area (i.e., $O(1)$ relative to $n$).

For $s=1, 2, 3$:
$G_{\text{max}}(n, s) = O(1)$ for $n > 75$.
This scaling satisfies $G_{\text{max}}(n, s) \le Cn$ for large $n$.

**Overall Conclusion for s=1, 2, 3:**
Since the sparsity condition must hold for *every* positive integer $n$, and we found that $G_{\text{max}}(n, s) = \Omega(n^2)$ for $n \le 75$, none of $s=1, 2, 3$ are sparse integers. Further investigation into larger values of $s$ is required to find the least sparse integer.

### [W2, round 1] Necessary Condition for Non-Sparsity
The total number of green cells is $G_{\max}(n, s) = s \cdot T_{\max}(n)$. For $s$ to be not sparse, we require $G_{\max}(n, s) = \omega(n)$.
The maximum possible number of turns is $T_{\max}(n) \le n^2/s$. If the green cell expansion allows for the grid to be tiled efficiently (which occurs when $s$ is small relative to $n$), $T_{\max}(n) \sim n^2/s$, yielding $G_{\max}(n, s) \sim n^2$. Since $n^2 = \omega(n)$, this means that any $s \ge 1$ satisfies the condition of being non-sparse, provided $n$ is sufficiently large.
The necessary condition for $G_{\max}(n, s) = \omega(n)$ is that $s$ must allow the tiling mechanism to dominate the local coverage limitations. Given $s$ is a positive integer, the least sparse integer $s$ is $s=1$.
