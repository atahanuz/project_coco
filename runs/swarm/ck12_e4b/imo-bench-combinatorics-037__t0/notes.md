We must show that if $S$ is constructed this way, then applying the closure rules (adding 11 or 20) to any element in $S$ results in an element already in $S$.

1. Let $n \in S$. By definition, $n \in C_k$ for some $k \in K$.
2. Thus, $n = k + g$ for some $g \in G$.
3. Consider $n+11$:
   $$n+11 = (k + g) + 11 = k + (g + 11)$$
   Since $G$ is the monoid generated by $\{11, 20\}$, $g + 11$ must also be in $G$.
   Therefore, $n+11 \in C_k$. Since $C_k \subseteq S$, $n+11 \in S$.
4. Similarly, consider $n+20$:
   $$n+20 = (k + g) + 20 = k + (g + 20)$$
   Since $g + 20 \in G$, we have $n+20 \in C_k$. Since $C_k \subseteq S$, $n+20 \in S$.

Since $S$ is a union of closed sets ($C_k$), $S$ itself is closed. This demonstrates that the closure operations applied to $S$ only generate elements within the orbits already chosen in $K$, confirming that $S$ cannot necessitate the inclusion of a new orbit $C_j$ where $j \notin K$.
