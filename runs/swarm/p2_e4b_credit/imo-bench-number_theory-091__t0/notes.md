### [W3, round 1]
Analysis of Condition 1 ($\gcd(a, b, c) = 1$ for $U=\{a, b, c\}$):
This condition implies a structural restriction on the prime factorizations of the elements. For any prime $p$, if $p$ were to divide all three elements $a, b, c$, then $p$ would divide $\gcd(a, b, c)$, contradicting the condition $\gcd(a, b, c)=1$. Therefore, every prime factor of any element in $U$ can divide at most two elements in $U$.

Smallest examples for $|U|=3$ satisfying this condition:
1. $U=\{1, 2, 3\}$. Product $m=6$. (Note: Allowing 1 helps find smaller candidates).
2. $U=\{2, 3, 4\}$. Product $m=24$.
3. $U=\{2, 3, 5\}$. Product $m=30$.
4. $U=\{6, 10, 15\}$. Product $m=900$. This set maximizes the shared factors (each prime 2, 3, 5 divides exactly two elements).

### [W4, round 1]
Re: Smallest Good Set of Size 4.
Analysis of necessary conditions for $|U|=4$: Let $U=\{u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4\}$. A key necessary condition derived from Constraint 1 ($\gcd(u_i, u_j, u_k)=1$) is that every prime factor of all elements in $U$ can divide at most two elements in $U$. 
Attempting to extend the known good set $U'=\{6, 10, 15\}$ (which satisfies $|U'|=3$) by adding a fourth element $d$ led to a contradiction. Specifically, Constraint 2 applied to the pair $\{6, d\}$ forces $d$ to be a multiple of 5, while Constraint 1 applied to $\{10, 15, d\}$ forces $d$ to be coprime to 5. 
This suggests that the smallest good sets might be of size 3, or that if size 4 solutions exist, they require a much more complex structure than those based on $p_j p_k$ products. Further searching for small structured sets is necessary, but the constraints are extremely tight.

### [W3, round 2]
[W3, round 2] Re-evaluation of $U=\{2, 3, 4\}$ (Product $m=24$):
1. **Condition 1 ($\gcd(a, b, c) = 1$):** For $U=\{2, 3, 4\}$, $\gcd(2, 3, 4) = 1$. This condition is satisfied.
2. **Condition 2 (Divisibility):** For every distinct pair $\{a, b\} \subset U$, there must exist $c \in U \setminus \{a, b\}$ such that $c | (a \cdot b)$.
    - Pair $\{2, 3\}$: Product $6$. Remaining element is $4$. $4 \nmid 6$. Condition 2 FAILS for this pair.
    - Pair $\{2, 4\}$: Product $8$. Remaining element is $3$. $3 \nmid 8$. Condition 2 FAILS for this pair.
Conclusion: $U=\{2, 3, 4\}$ is NOT a good set. The smallest examples listed in W3's round 1 must be checked against Condition 2.
