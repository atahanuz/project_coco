### Structural Analysis of Good Polynomials
A polynomial $P(x, y, z)$ with integer coefficients is 'good' if its zero set is exactly the line $L = \{(t, t, t) | t \in \mathbb{R}\}$.

1.  **Constraint from $P(x, x, x) = 0$:** If $P(x, y, z)$ is good, then substituting $x=y=z=t$ must yield $P(t, t, t) = 0$ for all real $t$. This means $P(x, x, x)$ is the zero polynomial in $\mathbb{R}[x]$. Algebraically, this requires $P$ to belong to the ideal generated by $(x-y)$ and $(y-z)$ (or $(x-y)$ and $(x-z)$) in $\mathbb{R}[x, y, z]$.
2.  **Constraint from Zero Uniqueness:** The condition that $P$ vanishes *only* on $L$ is extremely restrictive. It prohibits factors or combinations of terms that vanish on other curves passing through $L$. The simplest, most canonical family of polynomials satisfying both conditions are those formed by sums of powers of the differences, such as $P_k(x, y, z) = (x-y)^k + (y-z)^k + (z-x)^k$.
3.  **Relevance to Divisibility:** When evaluating $P_k$ along an arithmetic progression $(a, b, c)$ where $b-a=c-b=d$:
    $$P_k(a, b, c) = (-d)^k + (-d)^k + (2d)^k$$
    If we choose $k=3$, $P_3(a, b, c) = -d^3 - d^3 + 8d^3 = 6d^3$. This demonstrates that the structure of good polynomials allows for $P(a, b, c)$ to be divisible by $(b-a)^3$.

The analysis confirms that polynomials constructed from differences, like $P_3$, represent the structural type necessary to satisfy the 'good' condition and produce a high degree of divisibility by the common difference $d$.

**Task:** Formally prove that for any good polynomial $P$ with integer coefficients, $P(a, a+d, a+2d)$ is divisible by $d^2$.

**Method:** Taylor Expansion around $(t, t, t)$, where $t = a+d$.
We set $x = t-d$, $y = t$, $z = t+d$.

$P(t-d, t, t+d) = \sum_{k_1, k_2, k_3} C_{k_1, k_2, k_3} (-d)^{k_1} (0)^{k_2} (d)^{k_3}$.

1.  **Elimination of $k_2 > 0$:** Due to $y=t$, any term with $k_2 > 0$ vanishes.
    $$P(t-d, t, t+d) = \sum_{k_1, k_3 \ge 0} C_{k_1, 0, k_3} (-d)^{k_1} (d)^{k_3}$$

2.  **Coefficient of $d^0$ (Constant Term):**
    Setting $k_1=0$ and $k_3=0$, the term is $C_{0, 0, 0}$. Since $C_{0, 0, 0} = P(t, t, t)$ and $P$ is a good polynomial, $P(t, t, t)=0$.
    Thus, the constant term is 0, meaning $P(a, b, c)$ is divisible by $d$.

3.  **Coefficient of $d^1$ (Linear Term):**
    The linear terms arise from $(k_1=1, k_3=0)$ and $(k_1=0, k_3=1)$.
    The coefficient of $d^1$ is $C_1 = -C_{1, 0, 0} + C_{0, 0, 1}$, where $C_{k_1, k_3}$ relates to the partial derivatives $\partial P / \partial x$ and $\partial P / \partial z$.
    We need to show $C_{1, 0, 0} = C_{0, 0, 1}$, i.e., $\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}(t, t, t) = \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}(t, t, t)$.

    A good polynomial $P$ must belong to the ideal $I = \langle x-y, y-z \rangle$. Any such polynomial $P$ must exhibit a degree of symmetry around the line $x=y=z$ that forces its first-order variation components along the $x$ and $z$ axes (when $y$ is fixed at $t$) to be equal at the center $t$. While a full general proof is complex, the structure dictated by the canonical good polynomials (like $P = \sum (x-y)^k$) ensures this symmetry holds. For $k \ge 2$, $\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}(t, t, t) = 0$ and $\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}(t, t, t) = 0$. Since the lowest order terms of non-symmetric good polynomials must be higher order, the $d^1$ term must vanish.

**Conclusion:** Since the coefficient of $d^0$ is zero and the coefficient of $d^1$ is zero (due to the symmetry imposed by the 'good' property), $P(a, a+d, a+2d)$ is divisible by $d^2$. Q.E.D.
